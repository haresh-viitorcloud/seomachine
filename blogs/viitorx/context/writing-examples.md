## The Shared Article Skeleton

All five follow the same underlying shape. Use it as the default outline.

1. **Direct-answer opening**, two or three short paragraphs. States the problem and resolves the core question immediately. Contains the focus keyword and one internal link
2. **A plain definition or an at-a-glance table**, so the reader can orient and an AI system can extract
3. **The problem section**, framed in the buyer's terms and usually carrying one of the two external authority citations
4. **The substantive middle**, three to five H2 sections. Formats, frameworks, decision criteria, or numbered examples. H3s appear in some sections and not others
5. **Named project proof**, drawn from our own delivery
6. **A disqualifying or myth-busting section.** Where the technology does not apply, what it is commonly confused with, or which objections are fair
7. **CTA section**, project-backed, asking for a brief or a conversation
8. **Conclusion**, short
9. **FAQ**, four to six questions in natural prompt language

## Patterns Worth Copying

### Openings

Every one of the five opens the same way: **a concrete situation or a measured fact, then the problem, then what the article will do.** None opens with a definition of the industry, a rhetorical question, or scene-setting.

Four reusable moves:

- **Put the reader in a delivered scene**: the airport ground-breaking, the visitor walking past the artefact
- **State a first-hand observation in short sentences**: "The screen gets a glance. The hologram gathers a crowd"
- **Name the genre's weakness, then fix it**: "easy to find and hard to trust", "written for factory owners", "most advice starts with promotion"
- **Lead with the buyer's own contradiction**: heavy communication spend producing content visitors forget within hours

### Subheadings

Real examples from the corpus, all passing the newspaper-headline test in `context/style-guide.md`:

- "Why Visitors Walk Past Your Best Objects"
- "Most 'Holograms' You See in Malls Are Not Holograms"
- "Four Public Projects Where a 3D Hologram Replaced the Slide Deck"
- "The 1947 Physics Problem That Created the Hologram"
- "A Rollout Plan You Can Defend to Your Board"
- "The Three Objections Every Director Raises, Answered"
- "A Forest Grown From Guests' Phones"
- "Selling Homes That Did Not Exist Yet"
- "What a 3D Holographic Projection Project Involves Before Hardware Arrives"

Note what none of them do: no "Benefits of", no "Understanding", no "Everything You Need to Know", no bare topic labels.

### Correcting the Reader Before Selling

The hologram article's strongest move, worth reusing in any technology explainer. Dedicate a section to what the technology is *not*, and name the common fake:

> Most 'Holograms' You See in Malls Are Not Holograms

This costs nothing, disqualifies weaker vendors implicitly without naming anyone, and buys the technical credibility that the rest of the article spends.

### Answering Cost Without a Price

The rule in `context/features.md` is that content must not state or imply price. These articles satisfy cost intent anyway, by shifting to structure:

> There is no single price. Content production is usually the largest cost, followed by hardware class and installation. A small fan display costs a fraction of a hologram table or stage system.

> It depends on format. A single gallery WebAR pilot is the lowest entry point, while native apps and fixed installations cost more and suit permanent programmes. Content production, not hardware, is usually the largest line item. We scope against collection size, visitor volume, and your maintenance capacity.

Name the cost drivers, name the cheapest entry point, name what we scope against. No numbers.

### First-Party Framing

Short, unshowy, and load-bearing:

- "Our team built that model."
- "We build these systems for working museums."
- "We build these systems, so every explanation comes from delivered projects."
- "We have watched this in rooms of diplomats, ministers, engineers and school groups."
- "Everything above is our own delivery record."

### Natural Anchors

Real in-content anchors from the corpus. Every one is a phrase the sentence needed anyway:

- "holographic digital twin of Noida International Airport"
- "tracked visitors at two major art museums"
- "Networks of the Past exhibition at CSMVS"
- "immersive centre outside the regulated zone"
- "Tell us which gallery you would pilot first"
- "share a floor plan and a shortlist of objects with us"
- "tell us about the site and the goal"

No "click here", no "read more", no "you can check this".

### Bullet Discipline

Bold lead-in, colon, then explanation. Confirms the colon rule in `context/style-guide.md`:

> - **Reconstruction in place:** A damaged sculpture or a lost structure appears complete through the camera, at true scale, with no physical intervention on the object.
> - **Conservation safe interpretation:** Nothing touches the artefact. Light levels, humidity, and handling protocols stay exactly as your conservation team requires.

# Appendix: Full Text of Example 2

Reproduced from `/blog/ar-in-museums/` as the canonical full-length exemplar. Links are shown inline so anchor placement and distribution are visible. Body text only; template chrome removed.

AR in museums solves a problem every curator knows. A visitor stops at a 2,000 year old artefact, reads two lines on the label, and moves on. The object carries a century of scholarship. The visitor leaves with a caption. Augmented reality closes this gap by placing 3D reconstructions, translations, and guided stories directly over the physical collection, on a device the visitor already carries.

We build these systems for working museums. This article explains what holds up on a live gallery floor, which formats to choose, and how to plan a rollout your board will approve.

### What Is AR in Museums? A Plain Definition First

AR in museums is the use of augmented reality to overlay digital content, such as 3D models, animation, audio, and text, onto physical exhibits through a smartphone, tablet, kiosk, or headset. The object stays untouched while the story around it expands.

Augmented reality in museums keeps visitors in front of the real artefact, while virtual reality replaces the room entirely. Our guide to [immersive technology](https://viitorx.com/blog/immersive-technology-ar-vr-mr-explained/) compares AR, VR, and mixed reality in detail. For most galleries, AR is the practical choice because it runs on hardware visitors already own.

### Why Visitors Walk Past Your Best Objects

The attention problem is measured, not assumed. Researchers who [tracked visitors at two major art museums](https://www.artsy.net/article/artsy-editorial-long-people-spend-art-museums) found a mean viewing time of 27.2 seconds per artwork, with a median of 17 seconds. A repeat study fifteen years later recorded 28.6 seconds.

Wall text cannot fix this alone. It is static, printed in one language, and limited by panel space. AR interpretation gives every object a second layer the visitor can open, question, and share. The label states a fact. The layer holds attention.

### Five Ways AR Interpretation Works on the Gallery Floor

These are the AR exhibits formats that produce results for cultural institutions:

- **Reconstruction in place:** A damaged sculpture or a lost structure appears complete through the camera, at true scale, with no physical intervention on the object.

- **Layered storytelling:** Visitors tap through provenance, trade routes, and maker techniques instead of reading one fixed caption.

- **Multilingual access:** One AR layer serves every language your audience speaks, at a lower cost than reprinting signage for each exhibition.

- **Gamified learning:** Scavenger hunts and draw and discover tasks turn a school visit into an activity. Our list of [interactive museum exhibits](https://viitorx.com/blog/interactive-museum-exhibits/) covers twelve formats that pair well with AR.

- **Conservation safe interpretation:** Nothing touches the artefact. Light levels, humidity, and handling protocols stay exactly as your conservation team requires.

Most of these AR exhibits run on visitor phones, which keeps hardware budgets low and adoption high.

### What We Learned Inside CSMVS and MAP

Published examples usually come from institutions in Europe or North America. Our experience comes from two respected collections much closer to home, and the lessons apply to any team planning an augmented reality museum programme.

#### CSMVS, Mumbai: 250 Artefacts, Four Digital Experiences

For the Networks of the Past exhibition at Chhatrapati Shivaji Maharaj Vastu Sangrahalaya, we built four interactive experiences around more than 250 artefacts loaned from 14 museums across 6 countries. A tablet based digital twin of the Rosetta Stone let visitors tap each script and read its decipherment story. An interactive LED table connected Silk Road trade flows to the physical objects on display.

Dwell time at these digital touchpoints was measurably higher than at conventional displays, and a digital feedback station replaced paper forms with structured, real time data for curators. The complete build is documented in our [CSMVS digital experiences case study](https://viitorx.com/case-studies/digital-experiences-csmvs/).

Interactive digital experiences built by ViitorX for the CSMVS Networks of the Past exhibition.

#### MAP, Bengaluru: Draw a Shape, Find the Painting

At the Museum of Art and Photography, we built a kiosk that searches a collection of more than 20,000 artworks by shape. A visitor draws a spiral or a curve, and an image recognition engine returns the paintings that match. First time visitors explore the collection without knowing a single artist name.

### WebAR, Apps or Headsets: Decide Before You Build

Format choice sets your cost, adoption rate, and maintenance load. Most published guides skip this decision. We put it first in every scoping call:

- **WebAR:** Opens in the phone browser from a QR code, with no download. Best for temporary AR exhibits and broad audiences. Lowest friction, moderate visual fidelity.

- **Native app:** Higher graphics quality plus offline support. Best for a permanent augmented reality museum tour with repeat visitors. Requires downloads and ongoing updates.

- **Fixed installations:** Kiosks, LED tables, and projection surfaces. No visitor device needed, and the museum controls the full experience. Ideal for anchor galleries.

- **Headsets:** The deepest immersion at the highest cost per user. Suited to ticketed, staffed experiences, not free flow galleries.

A WebAR pilot is usually the fastest and cheapest way to test AR in museums before committing to an app.

### A Rollout Plan You Can Defend to Your Board

Treat AR in museums as a staged programme, not a single purchase. This is the sequence we recommend to directors and heritage trusts:

- **Scale on evidence:** Expand with the pilot numbers in hand. Public grant schemes for museum modernisation now fund digitisation and virtual museum components, which strengthens the proposal in front of any trust.

### FAQs

#### What does AR in museums cost?

It depends on format. A single gallery WebAR pilot is the lowest entry point, while native apps and fixed installations cost more and suit permanent programmes. Content production, not hardware, is usually the largest line item. We scope against collection size, visitor volume, and your maintenance capacity.

#### Do visitors actually use AR exhibits?

Yes, when friction is low. QR access with no forced download performs best. The Art Gallery of Ontario data above shows engagement rates above 80 percent when the experience is easy to open.

#### Will an augmented reality museum layer distract from the collection?

No. AR interpretation is contactless and reversible, and it directs attention toward the object. Museums also report visitors sharing screens and discussing exhibits together, which supports the social side of a visit.

#### How do we measure success?

Compare dwell time against your pre launch baseline. Then track sessions per visitor, completion rates, languages used, and repeat visits across the season.
