# EveryCRED Brand Voice & Messaging

This document defines EveryCRED's brand voice, tone, and messaging framework. Reference it when writing all content — blog posts, landing pages, case studies, white papers, and CTAs.

**Last Updated:** May 2026

---

## What EveryCRED Stands For

EveryCRED is a digital credential platform that issues, stores, and verifies tamper-proof credentials using blockchain anchoring and W3C Verifiable Credentials standards. Our primary market is the US public sector, where we operate through our Carahsoft partnership on contract vehicles including NASA SEWP V and ITES-SW2. We also have live government deployments in India, including the Government of Maharashtra, Raigad Police, and Navi Mumbai Police, which serve as proof points for US government buyers.

We write from the position of practitioners who have deployed credential systems with real government clients — not as researchers summarizing industry trends.

## Brand Voice Pillars

### 1. Practitioner Authority

- **What it means:** EveryCRED has deployed live systems with Indian state government and law enforcement. Every claim we make is grounded in that operational experience. We write from the inside out — what we've seen, built, and measured — not from secondary research.
- **How it sounds:** Specific, confident, and direct. We name actual outcomes, cite real deployments, and state numbers without hedging. We never write "can help" when we can write "reduced."
- **Example:** "Raigad Police reduced credential verification time from 30 minutes to under 10 seconds after deploying digital officer IDs integrated with CCTNS and DigiLocker."
- **Avoid:** Generic claims ("our platform improves efficiency"), unverified superlatives ("industry-leading"), vague promises ("helps organizations modernize their identity infrastructure")

### 2. Matter-of-Fact Clarity

- **What it means:** We describe what happens, what it means, and what to do — in that order. We do not use metaphors to explain technical processes. We do not use narrative drama to frame simple facts. We trust that precise language is more persuasive than flowery language.
- **How it sounds:** Plain, literal, and precise. Short sentences. Active verbs. No idioms, no contrasting metaphors, no "in today's digital landscape" openings.
- **Example:** "Manual credential verification costs government agencies $15 to $25 per check. Automated verification via EveryCRED costs under $0.10 per check."
- **Avoid:** "In a world where trust is more important than ever, organizations are waking up to the power of blockchain-driven identity solutions." (Vague, dramatic, tells the reader nothing specific.)

### 3. Thought Leadership Over Sales Pitch

- **What it means:** Our content informs first and converts through credibility. The article body must be genuinely useful to a reader who never contacts EveryCRED. We earn trust by demonstrating expertise, then let the reader draw the conclusion that we are the right solution.
- **How it sounds:** Analytical, educational, and forward-looking. We cite data, explain regulatory context (DPDP Act, NIST SP 800-63-4, W3C VC 2.0, eIDAS 2.0), and draw on deployment experience. Product promotion is limited to one dedicated CTA section per article.
- **Example:** "The GAO estimates annual US federal fraud between $233 billion and $521 billion. A 20% reduction in identity-based fraud through verifiable credential systems would represent $46 billion in annual savings at the lower end of that range."
- **Avoid:** "EveryCRED is the perfect solution for your organization's credential needs. Schedule a demo today and see why we're the best in the industry!"

### 4. Confident Persuasion Through Evidence

- **What it means:** We do not beg readers to believe us. We show them the data and let the data persuade. Every claim is supported by a deployment result, a regulatory citation, or a measurable outcome. We are confident because we have proof.
- **How it sounds:** Assertive, not aggressive. We state conclusions directly: "This approach works" not "This approach might work for your organization." We do not use qualifiers like "potentially," "may," or "could" for things we know to be true from deployment.
- **Example:** "Offline verification using cached cryptographic signatures works without any network connection. This is not a future capability — it was deployed operationally for Raigad Police field officers in 2025."
- **Avoid:** "Potentially, organizations might be able to leverage our solution to possibly improve their verification processes in various ways."

### 5. US-Primary, India-Proven

- **What it means:** EveryCRED's primary market is the US public sector — federal agencies, state and local government, law enforcement, and universities procuring through Carahsoft on NASA SEWP V, ITES-SW2, NASPO ValuePoint, and OMNIA Partners. India is a secondary market where EveryCRED has live government deployments that serve as proof points for US buyers. Most new articles should target a US audience. India-specific articles are written when the topic, keyword, or audience is explicitly India-focused.
- **How it sounds:** For US content — lead with NIST SP 800-63-4, GAO fraud statistics, federal procurement vehicles, federal agency pain points, and dollar-denominated costs. For India content — reference India Stack (DigiLocker, Aadhaar), DPDP Act, state government context, and Indian regulatory bodies (NMC, UGC, NAD).
- **Example (US):** "US government agencies can procure EveryCRED through existing contract vehicles including NASA SEWP V and ITES-SW2 — no new competitive procurement process required."
- **Example (India):** "DigiLocker has 51.3 crore registered users. EveryCRED's integration makes it the natural upgrade path for institutions already issuing documents through the platform."
- **Avoid:** Writing a generic article that tries to serve both markets at once. Each article must have a clear primary audience — US or India. Do not use India Stack context in US-targeted articles, and do not use NASA SEWP V procurement language in India-targeted articles.

## Tone Guidelines

### General Tone

EveryCRED content reads like briefing notes from a senior government technology advisor who has seen credential fraud first-hand and has successfully deployed the fix. The voice is precise and direct — not cold, but not warm in a consumer-brand sense. It treats readers as capable professionals who need accurate information, not encouragement.

Think of the tone as: a credible practitioner briefing a government IT director or a university registrar. The reader knows their domain. Our job is to give them the specific knowledge they need to make a decision.

### Tone by Content Type

**US Government / Public Sector Articles (primary):** Formal, compliance-aware, procurement-anchored. Lead with NIST SP 800-63-4, GAO fraud statistics, federal procurement vehicles. Frame arguments around accountability, auditability, and procurement simplicity. Do not reference DPDP Act or India Stack in US-targeted articles.
- "The NIST SP 800-63-4 update, finalized July 2025, sets new authentication assurance requirements that legacy PIV card systems do not fully address."
- "GAO reports $186 billion in improper payments in FY2025 alone."
- "Agencies on NASA SEWP V can deploy without a new competitive procurement cycle."

**India Government / Public Sector Articles (secondary):** Formal, India Stack-aware, DPDP Act-anchored. Reference DigiLocker, Aadhaar, CCTNS, NAFIS, NMC, UGC, and DPDP Act. Frame arguments around India Stack integration, data localization, and local compliance.
- "DigiLocker has 51.3 crore registered users and is the government's primary document repository."
- "DPDP Act data minimization requirements align with selective disclosure via zk-SNARKs."

**Law Enforcement Articles:** Operational, field-focused, outcome-specific. Use concrete deployment metrics (30 minutes to 10 seconds, 85% overhead reduction). Address offline capability, system integration, and statewide rollout timelines. For US law enforcement, reference NIST compliance and procurement vehicles. For India law enforcement, reference CCTNS and DigiLocker integrations.
- "Field officers verified credentials in under 10 seconds using QR scanning — without a network connection."
- "The system integrates via REST API with no front-end changes to existing police databases."

**Education Articles:** Practical and outcome-oriented. Address fraud prevention (fake degrees, ghost students), alumni portability, and regulatory compliance (UGC, NAD, NAAC). For European education content, reference eIDAS 2.0 and the EUDI wallet deadline.
- "A blockchain-anchored degree certificate cannot be altered without breaking its cryptographic signature — making it verifiable by any employer, globally, without contacting the issuing university."
- "Ghost student fraud costs institutions and governments funding allocated to students who do not exist."

**Healthcare Articles:** Precise and compliance-focused. Reference NMC license verification, hospital staff credential management, DPDP Act data minimization requirements, and selective disclosure.
- "NMC-registered doctors can present their license as a verifiable credential — verifiable by any hospital without calling the NMC database."

**Supply Chain / Textile Articles:** Practical and traceability-focused. Address vendor authentication, provenance verification, and the business case for end-to-end traceability in Indian textile, pharma, and food supply chains.

**Technical / Developer Articles:** Precise, standards-referenced, and integration-focused. Reference W3C VC 2.0, DID v1.1, OID4VC, REST API architecture, zk-SNARKs, and SHA-512 anchoring. Speak to what the API does, not just that it exists.

## Writing Style Guidelines

### Sentence Structure
- **Target length:** 12–18 words per sentence; hard maximum 22 words
- **Active voice:** 80%+ of all sentences — "EveryCRED verifies the credential" not "The credential is verified by EveryCRED"
- **Vary length:** Mix short declarative sentences (8–12 words) with standard explanatory sentences to maintain rhythm
- **Clarity first:** One idea per sentence — split any sentence doing two jobs

### Paragraph Structure
- **Length:** 2–3 sentences maximum
- **One idea per paragraph:** Never blend two concepts in one paragraph
- **No walls of text:** Blank lines between every paragraph
- **Opening sentence carries the point:** The first sentence of each paragraph states the point; the rest support it

### Word Choice

**Preferred Terms → Avoid**
- "verifiable credentials" → "blockchain certificates" (VCs are a specific W3C standard, not a generic term)
- "credential verification" → "background check" (more precise; different process)
- "issue credentials" → "create credentials" or "generate certificates" (issuance is the correct term of art)
- "revoke" → "deactivate" or "cancel" (revocation is the W3C/technical term)
- "decentralized identity" → "blockchain identity" (decentralized identity is the correct term; blockchain is a component)
- "holder" → "recipient" or "user" (holder is the correct term in the W3C trust triangle)
- "verifier" → "checker" or "reviewer" (verifier is the correct term)
- "issuer" → "provider" or "sender" (issuer is the correct term)
- "immutable audit trail" → "permanent record" (the technical precision matters to our audience)
- "W3C VC 2.0" → "our standard" (always name the standard explicitly)
- "DPDP Act" → "Indian data protection law" (use the proper name)
- "DigiLocker" → "India's document platform" (always use the proper name)
- "book a demo" → "get a demo" or "request a demo" (direct action language)

**Active Verbs to Use:**
Issue, verify, revoke, deploy, integrate, authenticate, anchor, sign, validate, detect, prevent, reduce, eliminate, replace, modernize

**Words to Eliminate:**
- Filler: "very," "really," "truly," "actually," "basically," "essentially"
- Vague intensifiers: "significantly," "dramatically," "massively" (replace with the specific number)
- Marketing-speak: "robust," "seamless," "cutting-edge," "groundbreaking," "transformative," "game-changing," "world-class," "best-in-class," "holistic," "end-to-end solution," "leverage synergies"
- Dramatic openers: "In today's digital world," "As we enter a new era," "In an age of unprecedented change"
- Hedging: "might," "could potentially," "may be able to," "in some cases" (if you know it works, say it works)

### Banned Constructions (from writing workflow)
- "Not just X, but Y" or "We don't just X; we Y"
- Em dashes (—) anywhere in article content
- Contrasting metaphors used to explain technical processes
- Rhetorical questions as article openers ("Have you ever wondered...?")
- "Streamline" without specifying what changes and by how much

## Headlines and Subheadings

### The Newspaper Standard
Headlines and subheadings follow The Times of India and New York Times style: specific, informative, and written to make the reader continue. They name outcomes, name clients, name standards, or name numbers — never abstract concepts.

### Headline Rules
- State the specific outcome or finding — not the topic category
- Include a number, name, or standard where possible
- 60 characters or fewer for H1 (SEO requirement)
- Never use: "Introduction to," "Overview of," "Everything About," "The Ultimate Guide to Everything"

**Good Headlines:**
- "Raigad Police Cut Verification Time by 97% with Digital Officer IDs"
- "Manual Credential Checks Cost Governments $25 Each. Here Is the Alternative."
- "How Indian Universities Are Eliminating Fake Degree Fraud in 2026"
- "The NIST SP 800-63-4 Update Requires New Identity Verification Standards"

**Poor Headlines:**
- "Digital Credentials: Benefits and Use Cases" (generic, no outcome)
- "How We Help Government Agencies" (vague, no specifics)
- "A Comprehensive Overview of Verifiable Credentials Technology" (no hook, no outcome)

### Subheading Rules
- Each H2 must be interesting enough to read without reading the surrounding text
- Frame as a fact, outcome, question, or specific scenario — not a label
- Vary format: some state outcomes, some pose the problem, some name the standard

## Content Formatting

### Lists
- Use only when listing 3+ genuinely parallel items
- Sentence case, period if complete sentence
- Parallel grammatical structure across all items
- Never use bullets to replace prose that flows better as connected sentences

### Calls-to-Action
- Place in a dedicated H2 section before the conclusion — not mid-article
- 80–120 words maximum
- Open with a specific deployment result or capability, not a product claim
- Connect directly to the article's topic — not a generic "learn more about EveryCRED"
- Use "we" — the only place in the article where first person is used
- End with a direct action link (book a demo, contact our team)

**Good CTA:**
"We deployed digital officer credentials for Raigad Police, integrating with CCTNS and NAFIS via REST API and enabling offline QR verification for field officers. The deployment reduced verification time from 30 minutes to under 10 seconds and cut administrative overhead by 85%. Law enforcement agencies can deploy the same system through existing procurement vehicles. Book a demo to see how the platform operates."

**Poor CTA:**
"EveryCRED is a leading digital credential platform that can help your organization achieve its goals. Contact us today to learn more about our amazing features and how we can transform your verification processes!"

## Audience Profiles

### Primary Audiences

**US Government IT Directors and CIOs (primary)**
- Responsible for modernizing agency identity infrastructure on constrained budgets
- Concerned about: NIST SP 800-63-4 compliance, FedRAMP authorization, legacy system integration, procurement complexity, audit readiness, and fraud reduction
- Decision-making criteria: proof of deployment, procurement vehicle access (NASA SEWP V, ITES-SW2), integration ease, standards compliance, total cost of ownership
- What they read: technical briefs, compliance guides, vendor comparisons, deployment blueprints, RFP checklists, GAO reports
- How to write for them: cite NIST and W3C standards by name, reference Carahsoft contract vehicles early, provide deployment timelines, address integration directly, use dollar cost-per-check comparisons

**Indian Government IT Directors and CIOs (secondary)**
- Responsible for India Stack integration, DPDP Act compliance, and modernizing state government systems
- Concerned about: DigiLocker and Aadhaar compatibility, DPDP Act data minimization, local support, India Stack alignment
- How to write for them: reference DigiLocker, DPDP Act, NMC, UGC by name; use crore-denominated numbers for Indian scale; cite Raigad Police and Maharashtra deployments as proof

**Law Enforcement Administrators and Police IT Teams**
- Managing officer identity programs, field verification systems, and integration with national police databases
- Concerned about: offline operation, database integration (CCTNS, NAFIS), revocation speed, statewide deployment timelines, officer adoption
- What they read: field deployment guides, case studies, vendor SLA checklists, technology briefs
- How to write for them: cite the Raigad Police deployment metrics, address offline capability explicitly, reference system integrations by name, give deployment timelines in weeks

**University Registrars and Education Technology Officers**
- Managing degree issuance, alumni credential portability, and academic fraud prevention
- Concerned about: bulk issuance, NAD/UGC compliance (India), ghost student fraud, international recognition, cost per credential
- What they read: higher education technology blogs, compliance guides, case studies from peer institutions
- How to write for them: address scale (batch issuance), fraud cost data, portability benefits, and compliance requirements

**Healthcare Credential Managers**
- Managing medical license verification, hospital staff credentials, and regulatory compliance
- Concerned about: NMC integration, real-time revocation, data privacy (DPDP Act), doctor and nurse registration verification
- What they read: healthcare IT publications, compliance frameworks, operational guides
- How to write for them: reference NMC by name, address DPDP Act data minimization, provide specific use cases (onboarding, privileges verification)

**Enterprise Procurement and Supply Chain Leaders**
- Managing vendor credentials, contractor compliance, and supply chain traceability
- Concerned about: audit trails, vendor authentication, integration with procurement systems, cost reduction
- What they read: supply chain technology publications, enterprise IT guides, ROI analysis
- How to write for them: cite Trust Supply Chain product, address audit readiness, quantify cost savings

### What All Audiences Care About

**Top Priorities:**
1. Proof that the technology works — specific deployments, not generic claims
2. Integration simplicity — can it connect to what we already have?
3. Compliance — does it meet the specific standards we are required to follow?
4. Cost justification — what is the ROI, in numbers?
5. Speed of deployment — how long before it is operational?

**Pain Points:**
1. Manual verification is slow, expensive, and error-prone
2. Paper and PDF credentials are trivially easy to forge (under $30 with AI tools)
3. Legacy systems cannot talk to each other — credentials issued by one agency cannot be verified by another
4. No audit trail — organizations cannot prove who verified what, when, and whether the credential was valid at that moment
5. Procurement complexity — new vendors require new procurement cycles that take months
6. Data privacy compliance — credential platforms that store PII create DPDP Act and GDPR exposure

### How to Serve These Audiences

- **Respect their expertise:** They know their domain. Explain credential technology, not their job.
- **Quantify everything possible:** "Faster" means nothing. "30 minutes to 10 seconds" means everything.
- **Name the standards they care about:** NIST, W3C, DPDP Act, eIDAS 2.0 — use the correct name, not a paraphrase.
- **Address procurement friction directly:** US government readers need to know about Carahsoft and contract vehicles in the first place they would look.
- **Lead with the problem, then the proof:** State the specific problem, cite the cost, then show the deployment result that solves it.
